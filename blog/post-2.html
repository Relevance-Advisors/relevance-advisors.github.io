Why We Mill Our Own Flour From North Carolina Grain

By Marguerite Sable · July 14, 2026

Most bakeries buy flour the same way: a distributor truck shows up with sealed bags of pre-milled, bleached, and enriched flour, and that's what goes into the mixer. We do that too, for volume — but a portion of every batch starts as whole wheat berries from a single grain farm two counties over, run through the stone mill in our back room the morning we bake.

Freshly milled flour behaves differently than flour that's been sitting in a bag for weeks or months. The bran and germ are still intact, which means more of the wheat's natural oils, enzymes, and flavor compounds make it into the dough instead of oxidizing away in storage. It also means the flour is more active — doughs made with fresh-milled flour ferment faster and need closer attention, which is part of why our 36-hour cold ferment gets adjusted by feel rather than a fixed schedule.

Sourcing locally also means we know exactly which field a given batch of wheat came from, and we can talk to the farm directly about how it was grown. It costs more than a bulk order from a national distributor, and it caps how much of our flour can realistically be self-milled given the size of our mill — but it's the difference our regulars notice most when they ask what makes the rye taste different here.
